# SOL 200 — The Psychology Behind the Characters: From Childhood to Mars

How we built the four crew members on psychological research, and what a teacher can do when they see one of them in a real student.

## At a glance

Every character in SOL 200 carries a wound from age eleven, a way of protecting themselves that they learned back then, a price they pay for that habit on Mars, and a chance to change. Each of these steps is grounded in published psychological research. This file also includes a practical guide for teachers: how to notice these patterns in students, how to respond, and when to refer a child to the school counselor.

## Introduction: children absorb everything

Children aged 7 to 12 don't learn only from lessons. They learn from everything they see and hear. Psychologist Albert Bandura showed that children learn behavior by watching others and imitating them (Bandura, 1977). A character a child loves in a game becomes a model for how to handle mistakes, fear and conflict.

So we built every character on one rule: **what a child lives through at eleven becomes the decision they make as an adult.**

## How to read this file

Each character follows the same order: **the wound**, then **what the science says**, then **how it shows up on Mars**, then **how they change**, then **what the child learns**.

## Damon, the Body: the boy nobody saw

**The wound.** Damon is the second son. His older brother Ethan is a gifted, popular student, and every dinner conversation is about him. Damon's parents love him, but nobody really notices him, so he learns that people only see him when he does something dangerous.

**What the science says.** Psychologists call this "parental differential treatment": a child receiving less warmth from their parents than a sibling does. A meta-analysis that combined dozens of studies found that a child who receives less warmth and more hostility than their sibling shows more behavior problems, and that the link is strongest for problems the child acts out in behavior ([Eradus et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1037/fam0001194)). That is exactly Damon: the boy who felt like less, and started showing it through risk.

**How it shows up on Mars.** After Ethan dies, Damon carries a feeling that he is living his brother's dream and doesn't deserve it. Psychology has a name for this: the impostor phenomenon, the inability to own one's achievements, with a fear of being exposed as a fraud. It was first described by psychologists Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes in 1978 ([Clance & Imes, 1978](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0086006)), and later research showed it affects men too. This is why Damon always says "I've got this," and why he refuses help: "If I ask him for help, everyone will know I don't deserve to lead."

**How he changes.** In Chapter 2, the player chooses: does Damon take all the credit, as he did with June as a boy, or admit that Marcus advised him? In Chapter 5, Damon talks about Ethan for the first time, and he stops saying "I'm the commander" so much.

**What the child learns.** Someone who looks strong may carry a sadness he never talks about, and real courage isn't taking risks; it's admitting that you need other people.

## Marcus, the Mind: the plan nobody read

**The wound.** At eleven, Marcus spent weeks building a power budget for a science project. Then his friend Theo told the judges the idea was his. Marcus said nothing, and the friendship ended in silence. He learned to work alone and to hide his anger.

**What the science says.** Psychologists James Gross and Oliver John distinguish two ways of handling emotions. The first is reappraisal: changing how we think about the situation that triggers the emotion. The second is suppression: hiding the outward signs of the feeling. Research shows that suppression hides the feeling on the outside but doesn't reduce it on the inside. Their studies found that people who suppress feel fewer positive emotions yet more negative ones, and that suppression is linked to worse relationships ([Gross & John, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.85.2.348)).

**How it shows up on Mars.** Marcus was right about his plan, but he never corrected the training report in front of the committee. And the silence isn't his alone. Researchers Morrison and Milliken describe a pattern in which members of an organization hold back information about potential problems, and they call it "organizational silence" ([Morrison & Milliken, 2000](https://doi.org/10.2307/259200)). This silence can weaken decision-making and error correction, and damage trust and morale.

**How he changes.** In Chapter 3, Marcus writes a full report, and adds a line about his own mistake: that he stayed silent too. This is where he learns that being right isn't enough.

**What the child learns.** Staying silent doesn't fix a mistake. Marcus worked hard on his project, his friend took the credit, and Marcus said nothing and hid his anger. But the anger didn't go away; it stayed with him for years. When he saw a mistake in the training report, he stayed silent again, and the whole crew paid the price. So if someone takes credit for your work, or you see something wrong, don't keep it inside. Calmly tell someone you trust what happened. The honest word at the right time fixes what silence never can.

## Diego, the Psyche: the joke that became a shield

**The wound.** At eleven, Diego moved with his family from Mexico to Houston, and was bullied at his new school. He tried fighting back and got punished. He tried staying quiet and the bullying went on. He tried a joke, and everyone laughed. So he held on to the joke.

**What the science says.** Bullying is not a small thing in a child's life. According to a scientific review by psychology professor Dieter Wolke and his colleague, one in three children say they have been bullied at some point, and 10 to 14% experience chronic bullying lasting more than six months ([Wolke & Lereya, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4552909)). The authors argue that bullying should be treated as a significant risk factor and a child safeguarding issue. In a study of two groups of children in two countries, being bullied by peers had generally worse long-term effects on young adults' mental health ([Lereya et al., 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4580734)).

Not all humor is the same, either. Psychologist Rod Martin and his colleagues describe four humor styles. Some humor makes others laugh and brings people closer; other humor is used to put oneself down, or as defensive denial. The first kind is healthy; the second is not (Martin et al., 2003).

**How it shows up on Mars.** Diego finds a leak in the water system. He puts a temporary patch on it and hides it from the crew. When Marcus starts to suspect something, Diego distracts everyone with jokes so nobody gets close to the problem. Here the joke is no longer humor; it has become a way to escape and to hide the truth.

**How he changes.** In Chapter 4, he finally remembers the sentence that was lost in the noise of the engine, and the player hears it in full: his father used to say, "Laugh after you fix it." This is where the joke turns from a shield into healthy humor that comes after facing the problem, not instead of it.

**What the child learns.** Hiding a problem doesn't solve it. Diego found the leak, hid it, told no one, and kept everyone busy with jokes so they wouldn't notice, and the leak got worse. When you face a problem, turning people's attention away from it doesn't make it disappear. Tell someone who can help you, then laugh once the problem is solved, just as Diego's father said.

## Layla, the Soul: the broken jar

**The wound and the cure together.** Layla is the only character who received the right response as a child. She broke her grandmother's jar and hid the pieces. Her grandmother didn't punish her. She said, "It's okay, habibti. What happened, happened. Come, let's fix it together." Then she planted mint in the glued jar.

**What the science says.** Developmental psychologist Ed Tronick and pediatrician Claudia Gold argue that messiness in relationships is not only acceptable but essential to our social and emotional development. What matters is not that mismatches and mistakes never happen, but that repair follows them. Working through this cycle of mismatch and repair in everyday life helps us build deep, trusting relationships and resilience in times of stress (Tronick & Gold, 2020). That is exactly what the grandmother did: the mistake happened, and then the repair came, together.

**How it shows up on Mars.** When her grandmother fell ill, Layla's soul dimmed. She read the incomplete training report and stayed silent. Then her grandmother died and left her seeds and the same jar. Psychology has a theory called "continuing bonds," put forward by Klass, Silverman and Nickman in 1996. It says we don't need to let go of the people we lose; instead we go through a period of adjustment in which we redefine our relationship with them (Klass et al., 1996). On Mars, Layla plants the seeds and names the plant "Hope," and her grandmother stays with her.

**How she changes.** In Chapter 5, Layla confesses to Damon what she kept silent about, and says her grandmother's words to him. The lesson she learned as a girl, she now gives to someone else.

**What the child learns.** If you make a mistake and hide it, you stay scared and alone; if you tell someone who loves you, they will help you fix it. And something broken can be repaired and become beautiful again, like the jar.

## The whole crew: when silence becomes shared

Damon wasn't the only one who got the training accident wrong. All four stayed silent, each for their own reason. So the real question in the story isn't "who is to blame?" but "can the crew tell the truth?"

- **Psychological safety:** Harvard professor Amy Edmondson describes it as a shared belief among team members that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking. In her study of 51 work teams, psychological safety was linked to learning behavior, and learning was what connected psychological safety to team performance ([Edmondson, 1999](https://doi.org/10.2307/2666999)). The Team meter in the game measures exactly this.
- **The power of speaking up:** Across many studies, psychologist James Pennebaker found that when people write about their emotional experiences, their physical and mental health improve significantly. He argues that the act of disclosure itself is a powerful therapeutic agent ([Pennebaker, 1997](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1997.tb00403.x)). This is what happens in the first ending: the four of them talk, and the crew heals. In the second ending, the silence continues, and they lose each other.

## For teachers: when you see one of these characters in a student

Every character in the game is a real child who might be sitting in your classroom. This section doesn't turn a teacher into a therapist. It helps you notice early, respond in a way that doesn't deepen the wound, and know when to refer a child to the counselor. For using the game in a class, see the [Guide for Teachers, School Counselors and Parents](PARENTS_AND_TEACHERS.md).

### A student like Damon: takes risks to be seen

**What you might notice:** Does risky or showy things to get attention. Always says "I can do it" and refuses help. Finds it hard to admit mistakes. May often be compared to a brother or sister.

**What helps:**
- Notice him when he acts calmly, not only when he shows off, so he learns there is another way to be seen.
- Make asking for help normal in your class, and ask for help yourself in front of them sometimes.
- Give him a leadership role that requires listening, like gathering his group's opinions before a decision.

**What to avoid:** Comparing him with his siblings or classmates, and praise that rewards only speed and risk.

**Something to say:** "I noticed you asked your partner before you decided. That's what a leader does."

### A student like Marcus: stays silent and hides his anger

**What you might notice:** Quiet and precise, prefers working alone. Gets frustrated with group work, or withdraws from it. Says nothing when someone else takes credit for his work.

**What helps:**
- In group work, ask each student to say what they contributed, so nobody's effort gets lost.
- Give him a private way to tell you what's bothering him, like a talk after class or a note he can leave you.
- Acknowledge his feeling first: "You have every right to be upset."

**What to avoid:** Brushing it off with "it's no big deal," or forcing him into a public confrontation in front of the class.

**Something to say:** "Your work matters. If you ever feel someone took it, come and tell me."

### A student like Diego: distracts everyone with jokes

**What you might notice:** Turns every serious moment into a joke, especially when asked about a problem of his own. Avoids any confrontation. May be a new student, or different in accent or circumstances.

**What helps:**
- Don't punish the joke alone. Ask calmly, in private, what's behind it.
- Watch his relationships with classmates, because the joke may be a defense against bullying you don't see.
- Welcome new students, and help them find a place in a group.

**What to avoid:** Laughing along and moving on, so he learns that a joke closes the topic, or embarrassing him in front of the class.

**Something to say:** "That was a good joke. And after it, I'd like to hear from you: how are you really doing?"

**If you suspect bullying:** Don't leave it to a class discussion. Intervene immediately, separate the students involved, make sure everyone is safe, then follow your school's policy ([StopBullying.gov](https://www.stopbullying.gov/respond/index.html)). Responding quickly and consistently tells students that bullying is not acceptable.

### A student like Layla: hides her mistakes out of fear

**What you might notice:** Hides her mistakes or lies out of fear of punishment. Or suddenly withdraws and goes quiet, especially after losing someone close or after someone close falls ill.

**What helps:**
- When she admits a mistake, respond as the grandmother did: calmly, then fix it together.
- If you learn of a loss or illness in her family, check on her gently, and let her talk about the person she loves.

**What to avoid:** A harsh reaction to her confession, because it teaches a child to hide next time.

**Something to say:** "It's okay. What happened, happened. Come, let's fix it together."

### General rules for every case

- **Talk in private**, not in front of the class.
- **Listen more than you advise.** A child needs to be heard before being guided.
- **Don't diagnose.** Your role is to notice and support, not to put a label on a child.
- **Refer the child to the school counselor** if their sadness or withdrawal persists, if their behavior changes suddenly, or if they talk about loss or about being hurt. If they talk about hurting themselves, refer them immediately, and inform their family in the way your school normally does.

## How we protect the child who plays

Because children absorb everything, we followed fixed principles in the writing:

- **No death or violence on screen:** The story has real loss, but we never show the moment of death, only the absence and its effect: an empty chair, a bag of seeds.
- **Bullying told safely:** Words, looks and exclusion, with no on-screen violence and no harmful detail.
- **A mistake brings help:** Even if the player hides the pieces of the jar, the grandmother comes with help, not punishment.
- **The hero isn't perfect:** We didn't make Damon's recklessness heroic; we showed its cost.
- **All feelings are allowed:** Sadness, fear and anger show on the characters' faces. What matters is what we do with them.
- **Failure doesn't stop the journey:** A meter drops and the story goes on.
- **The lesson comes from the story, not from a lecture.**
- **No personal data:** The game is free and needs no account.

## Our limits

The game is not psychological therapy or a clinical tool, and it has not yet been tested in a formal study with children. The sources in this file are the foundation we built the characters on, not results we have measured. Only Chapter 1 is currently playable; the childhood scenes are part of the written story in Chapters 2 to 5.
