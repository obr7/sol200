# SOL 200 — Guide for Teachers, School Counselors and Parents

## Who this guide is for

This guide is for teachers, school counselors and parents who want to use SOL 200 with children aged 7 to 12. It turns play into a lesson: the child plays, stops to think about their decisions, then connects them to their own life.

**Play the game:** https://obr7.github.io/sol200/

**Printable worksheet:** [Decision Map (PDF)](decision-map.pdf)

## The game in one minute

The child plays Damon, commander of the first mission to grow food on Mars. A dust storm is coming, and the crew does not agree on what to do. The child explores the base, plays mini-games built on NASA science, and makes two hard decisions:

1. Go out into the storm to clean the solar panels, or stay inside and share the little power left?
2. Fix the oxygen machine alone, or ask Marcus, who knows the machine better, for help?

Every decision has a cost on three meters: Safety, Mission and Team. The Team meter decides the ending.

## What you need

- One device for every two students, with a web browser and an internet connection.
- The game link. Nothing to install, no account needed.
- A printed copy of the [Decision Map](decision-map.pdf) for each pair.
- Headphones if possible, because the game has voice and music.

## The 45-minute class

**Warm-up: 5 minutes.**
Ask the students: "If you were alone on Mars and couldn't ask anyone on Earth, how would you make decisions?" Then tell them they will play a mission commander, and that the goal is not only to win, but to notice what their decisions cause.

**Playing in pairs: 20 minutes.**
Two students play on one device. There is one rule: nobody presses a decision button until both partners agree out loud. If they disagree, each explains their reason, then they decide together.

This rule is the heart of the class. It makes the child live what the crew lives in the game: listening, persuading, and sometimes giving way.

**The Decision Map: 10 minutes.**
When the chapter ends, each pair fills in the Decision Map together.

**Discussion: 10 minutes.**
The whole class discusses some of the questions below. It helps to start with: "Who chose to go outside? Who chose to stay in?" and then compare what happened to each group.

**After the class.**
Students replay at home or in a later class and choose the other path this time, then compare the two results.

## The Decision Map

A simple worksheet with four parts for each decision:

- **Our choice:** what did we choose in the storm decision, and in the oxygen machine decision?
- **Why?** What made us choose it?
- **What happened next?** What changed on the meters, and who in the crew was affected?
- **How did the crew feel?** Draw the face of Marcus, Diego or Layla after the decision, and write one word for how they feel.

At the bottom: "If we played again, would we choose the same? Why?"

The PDF has a page that explains the three steps of deciding together (stop, talk, agree), a worked example, and a blank page to print.

## Discussion questions

The questions go from simple to deep. Choose what fits your class.

**Level 1: What happened?**

- What two decisions did you make?
- What happened to Diego or the garden after the storm decision?
- What did Marcus's face look like at the end of the chapter?

**Level 2: Why?**

- Why was it hard for Damon to ask Marcus for help?
- Was there a completely right choice? Why not?
- Why does the Team meter decide the ending, and not the Mission meter?

**Level 3: And you?**

- Have you ever needed help but didn't ask for it? What were you afraid of?
- When is asking for help brave?
- If you were Marcus, how would you feel if the commander ignored your advice?

## Role-play: Damon and Marcus

A 10-minute activity for a second class or a counseling session.

Two students stand in front of the class. One is Damon, the other is Marcus, at the moment the oxygen machine stops.

1. Damon says his inner thought out loud: "If I ask him for help, everyone will know I don't deserve to lead."
2. Marcus says what he feels inside: how does it feel when nobody asks him?
3. The two students swap roles and play the scene again.

At the end, ask the class: "What one sentence, said by either of them, would have changed everything?"

The goal is for the child to see the moment from both sides, and to understand that fear, not stubbornness, stopped Damon.

## For school counselors

The game gives children a safe distance. They talk about Damon's fear and Marcus's anger, not their own. Many children find it easier to talk about a character than about themselves, and then move closer to their own experience step by step.

**Activity: the four sides.**
Explain that the four crew members are four sides of every person:

- **Damon is the Body:** action and movement.
- **Marcus is the Mind:** thinking and planning.
- **Diego is the Psyche:** our feelings, our relationships, and what we hide.
- **Layla is the Soul:** meaning, hope and values.

Then ask each student: "Which side of you was tired today? Which side was strongest?" This can become a regular opening for small-group sessions.

**Topics the game opens for small groups:**

- Being afraid of looking weak when asking for help.
- Hidden anger when nobody listens, as happened to Marcus.
- Hiding pain behind jokes, as Diego does.

**When a student reminds you of a character.** For signs to watch for, what helps, what to avoid and what to say to a student who acts like Damon, Marcus, Diego or Layla, see [For teachers](PSYCHOLOGY.md#for-teachers-when-you-see-one-of-these-characters-in-a-student) in the psychology file.

## Adapting by age

**Ages 7 to 9**

- The child plays with a teacher or a family member, not only with another child.
- The Decision Map is shortened to two drawings: what I chose, and the character's face afterwards.
- The discussion stays at Level 1, with one question from Level 3.
- Instead of role-play, ask one simple question: "What would you say to Damon if you were his friend?"

**Ages 10 to 12**

- Students play in pairs, as in the class above.
- They fill in the full Decision Map.
- The discussion reaches Level 3.
- After class, a student can write a short message from Damon to Marcus.

## What to watch for while they play

These signs help you see what the child is learning. They are not grades.

- Does the student listen to their partner before pressing the button?
- Do they explain the reason for their choice, or just say "because"?
- Do they notice the characters' faces when they change?
- Do they accept a result that isn't what they wanted, or want to replay straight away to "win"?
- Do they connect the game to something in their own life during the discussion?

## For parents at home

- Play with your child, and let them lead. Don't tell them what is right.
- After playing, one question at dinner is enough, such as: "What was the hardest decision?" or "If you were Damon, would you have asked for help?"
- If your child says they made the "wrong" choice, remind them that the game has no perfect choice, and that what matters is what they learned from the result.

## Important note

SOL 200 is a learning tool. It is not psychological therapy, and it does not diagnose anything.

The story touches on fear, anger and loss gently, in a way that fits the age group. But if you notice that a child is very sad or stays sad, or talks about losing someone close or about being hurt, don't rely on class discussion alone. Talk with the child privately, refer them to the school counselor or a specialist, and inform their family in the way your school normally does.
